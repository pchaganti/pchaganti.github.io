# The Lagoon at Pyrrha

He had no patience for shadows on cave walls when the tide went out. At the lagoon of Pyrrha, the shallow water smelled of sulfur and rotting eelgrass over white limestone. Aristotle knelt with his tunic pinned above his shins, paring back the belly of a dogfish with a dull iron blade. Where his master looked upward toward pure geometry, the Macedonian preferred the slime of the actual. He cataloged the hectocotyl arm of the octopus and traced the chambered stomach of a heifer. He counted the legs of a shield bug—getting them wrong now and then, because human eyes grow tired in the noon glare—trusting the salt on his thumb far more than the pristine void of the Forms.

His thought moves with the gait of a man measuring an orchard by paces. Everything must find its place in the cedar chest: the genus, the species, the difference that carves one from another. A table is wood shaped to an end; an eye is an eye only while it can see. Remove sight, and it becomes a stone eye, an eye only in name, like a painted dog. This is not callousness; it is an immense, unblinking courtesy paid to the surfaces of the earth. He listened to the fishermen talk of the seasonal madness of cuttlefish and set their gossip down beside the constitutions of one hundred and fifty-eight city-states. An assembly of citizens bickering under an awning was no less an animal than the stingray resting in gray silt. Both moved toward an end, or stumbled short of it.

Beneath the olive trees of the Lyceum, he walked while he taught. Motion was his obsession—how the dry acorn remembers the timber of the oak, how potentiality aches toward act like tallow waiting for a spark. Yet he lived always on the margin of the polis, an alien without a vote, shielded by Macedonian spears until the spears snapped. When Athens turned on him after Alexander choked on fever in Babylon, he packed his papyrus rolls and took ship for Chalcis, remarking that he would not give the Athenians a second chance to sin against philosophy. Socrates swallowed hemlock because he belonged to the city’s hearth; Aristotle fled because an observer must stay alive to see what washes ashore next.

In his will, preserved by a later biographer, he remembers the household slaves: none are to be sold; some are to be freed when they come of age. There is four minas of silver for his daughter’s dowry, and a quiet instruction to bury the bones of his wife Pythias beside his own, as she had asked before she died. Even the afterlife of the master of logic was an affair of pine planks, a modest sum of coin, and two skeletons laid side by side in dry clay, keeping their specific weights.
