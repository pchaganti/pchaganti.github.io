# The Geometry of Salt and Lime

The chamfer of the Eixample was an engineering concession to turning radiuses that steam trams never fully claimed. Ildefons Cerdà clipped forty-five degrees off the corners of every square block so that sea air might circulate between the bedrooms of clerks and dockhands, though what settles in those open pockets now is scooter exhaust and the burnt-fat smell of fried cuttlefish. Walking inland from the harbor, the city flattens itself against the northern ridge like a salted hide nailed to a board. The paving tiles—the grey cement *panots* stamped with four-petaled flowers—hold a film of yellow dust and rain that evaporated before reaching the storm drains.

South of the grid, the older quarter refuses the square. Its lanes are ravines cut through Montjuïc sandstone, a porous, iron-flecked stone that drinks dampness out of the maritime air and crusts white when the wind turns dry. In the cloister of the cathedral, thirteen white geese circle an enclosure of green water, retained by an ancient municipal ordinance for a child martyr whose flesh was raked with wool-carding combs. Their bills are bright as orange rinds against the flagstones. If you lean against the Roman foundation below the choir, the masonry is slick, chilled by subterranean moisture that still leaks past broken amphorae embedded in the clay.

It is customary to treat the city’s eccentric masonry as an escape into organic ornament, forgetting that it began as an argument about gravity and the load-bearing limits of thin brick. Gaudí determined the pitch of his arches by stringing lead birdshot from hemp cords, observing their inverted catenary curves in mirrors tilted on the floor. His basilica smells of hydraulic lime and crane grease, with the dry chalk of crushed brick hanging in the scaffolding. The spires do not dissolve into spirit; they are anchored by steel dowels sunk into hard marl. At midday, the sunlight passing through the southern clerestory stains the concrete piers a heavy, dried-plum red.

Toward evening, the breeze drops from the Collserola ridge, smelling of pine scrub and wild fennel before crossing the zinc roofs of the freight yards. On the apartment terraces, clotheslines run between brick vent pipes, loaded with graying undershirts that flap once before the marine humidity pins them flat. A man in an unbuttoned vest leans his forearms against a rusted iron rail, tapping loose flake tobacco onto an enamel saucer. In the cross street below, the traffic lamps cycle through amber in empty, synchronized lines, illuminating curbs where the air tastes faintly of sea salt and pulverised slate.
