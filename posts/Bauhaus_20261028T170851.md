# Geometry Against the Snow

A nickel-plated steel tube bent around an empty space changes the posture of the person sitting down. In 1925, Marcel Breuer stripped the German parlor of its horsehair upholstery and heavy brocades soaked in cigar smoke and mutton fat. The chair became a drawing in air: two loops of cold pipe, a strip of paraffin-waxed canvas pulled taut enough to drum on. One did not sink into it to digest an empire; one sat alert, suspended four inches above linoleum. The room ceased to be a vault for ancestral bric-a-brac and became an envelope of unencumbered light.

Before it hardened into an international trademark, the school was poor, cold, and smelled of turpentine and raw garlic. In Weimar, students shaved their heads and studied the friction of boxwood against zinc, trying to scrape away the Victorian curlicue. What they sought was not style, but the naked spine of utility. To join pane glass to iron without an ornamental collar required an almost monastic hygiene. A teapot was reduced to a brass hemisphere, an ebony grip that would not burn the fingers, and a spout placed low so boiling water poured without sputtering. If there was a spiritual ambition, it lay in this refusal to flatter: the pine table was planed flat, its joints left bare, proud of its screws.

In Dessau, Walter Gropius built walls that were entirely window. Standing on the workshop floor, an apprentice looked out at the pine flats of Saxony through three stories of industrial glazing, held by thin iron mullions painted graphite gray. Dust had nowhere to hide; petty domestic secrets had no dark alcove to fester. It was an architecture designed on the innocent presumption that if human beings lived without shadows, they would govern themselves without cruelty. Yet a building made of clear glass offers no defense against men with crowbars and boots. When local authorities padlocked the Dessau studios in 1932, the city councilmen complained that the flat roofs were an affront to German winter, an alien geometry unfit for snow.

Dispersed into exile, that radical clarity was soon absorbed by Manhattan boardrooms and airport lounges—the cool, unblinking surfaces of administrative power. But the initial impulse was humbler, stranger, and far more precarious. In a small photograph from 1928, an anonymous student sits on an unpainted stool, holding a single chicken egg against a square of black cardboard. He is not admiring its beauty; he is measuring the curve of the shell against the daylight, trying to calculate how much load a thin calcified skin can bear before it fractures.
