# The Broad Shoulders

He had been a wrestler before he took to the wax tablets, broad-shouldered enough that his trainer abandoned the family name and called him for his width. One pictures the heel dug into Attic sand, the heavy film of olive oil and grit scraped off the forearm with a curved bronze strigil. Wrestling, however, leaves no lasting grievance with the city. What ruined him for ordinary politics was the cup of pale hemlock carried to an ugly stonemason who refused to leave the market stalls. When the poison crept up Socrates’ shins—hardening the flesh into something cold as unquarried marble before it reached the diaphragm—the student retreated to preserve an old man’s breath in ink.

Every dialogue is an act of staged revenge. He warned that the poets should be escorted to the city gate and crowned with fillets of wool before being expelled, yet he wrote like an incurable dramatist. His men sit on low benches, gather their cloaks against an evening wind off the Piraeus, and bicker over whether a bench made of pine contains less truth than the form of a bench. He argued that the written word was an abandoned child, dumb when questioned, incapable of defense; then he filled rolls of dried papyrus with that silence. Even his cave smells of damp rock and greasy tallow: prisoners pinned by iron bands, their eyes watering in the glare of a ditch-fire while puppeteers lift tin shapes of horses into the dark.

His doctrine of the Forms is not a bloodless geometry; it is a survivor’s desperation. If the skin of an Athenian boy must slacken, and if the assembly can gather on a Tuesday to vote death to its wisest citizen, there must be a country where numbers do not rot. He wanted a world untouched by August heat, where a triangle retains its three clean angles without charcoal smudging under a thumb. It is the austere longing of an exile who has watched everything mortal decay or betray itself.

Outside Athens, along the dry gravel of the Ilissus, the water runs ankle-deep under a plane tree. In the *Phaedrus*, the cicadas keep up their thin, dry clicking while two men talk of divine madness. Plato was not lying beside that stream. He was in a shuttered room, leaning over a desk with a small iron blade, shaping the split nib of a reed and trying to recover the exact cadence of a dead man’s laugh.
