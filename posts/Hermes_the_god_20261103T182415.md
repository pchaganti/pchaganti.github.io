# The God of the Ditch

Before he was given wings for his ankles, he was a heap of rough limestone at the edge of a wheat field. A traveler tossed a stone onto the cairn to clear his path, and in that modest act of clearing, a god collected bulk. He belongs to the margin where the surveyor’s cord slacks. When two farmers argue over an olive tree whose roots drink from one ditch while its branches drop black fruit into another, Hermes is already sitting on the low dry-stone wall between them, paring his thumbnail with a bronze blade.

He was born at dawn; by noon he had killed a mountain tortoise, scraped its scooped shell clean with a dry reed, and stretched cowhide over the hollow to make a box that sang. He did not invent the instrument out of sorrow, but out of curiosity regarding tension and gut. By twilight he had stolen his brother’s red heifers, driving them backward into the dark mouth of a cave so their tracks pointed toward the open grass. It is this reverse stride that defines him. Apollo demands an unbearable daylight; Hermes prefers the cool shadow behind a sheepcote, where a barter can be concluded before the dust has settled on the road. He does not lie so much as he allows language to slip sideways, like an axle greased with lard.

He is the only Olympian who carries dirt beneath his nails and road-clay on his shins. Zeus sits heavy on his cedar bench; Hermes walks down to the river where the unburied wait on the cold slate bank. His task there is quiet, almost clerical. He does not weigh their sins, nor does he weep over children whose breath still smells of barley porridge. He simply takes an old sailor by the crook of the elbow, guides him past the dog, and steps over the black puddles without wetting his leather. A good guide never hurries, because hurry implies that distance has the upper hand.

In the market at Piraeus, the grain dealers know him by a lead weight filed down on its underside. A lucky find in the dirt—a dropped obol, a fig split by wasps—is called *hermaion*, his casual gift. It carries no moral instruction. It is merely the remainder after the balance has settled: an accidental surplus left in the dust when two men believe they have divided a sack of salt down to the final grain.
