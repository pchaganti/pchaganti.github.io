# The Flesh of the Snail

In the winter quarters at Carnuntum, Marcus Aurelius dipped his reed into ink made of lampblack and vinegar. Outside the leather walls of his tent, the mud along the Danube was freezing into ridged ruts that lamed the pack mules. He did not write to comfort a court or erect an altar to his own patience. He wrote to keep his bile down, to dismantle the daily rot of grievance before it could calcify. A purple consular cloak, he reminded himself by lamplight, was only sheep’s wool soaked in the gore of a shellfish. Vintage wine was fermented grape juice; roast fowl was a carcass. If a man stripped the adjectives from the world, things could no longer frighten him with their grandeur or their insults.

Epictetus, who walked with a twisted hip after an owner broke the bone, understood this subtraction from beneath. He did not teach beneath marble colonnades; he spoke in an ill-heated room that smelled of mutton tallow and boiled greens. His students were young men anxious about honors, terrified of a cold glance from a magistrate. He did not urge them to become statues. He merely asked them to sort their belongings from what was merely lent. When an earthen pitcher slips from a wet hand and breaks upon the flagstones, the sound is brief. It is the imagination, not the clay, that howls. To mourn the vessel is to demand that baked mud possess the durability of granite.

The labor of this philosophy is dry, repetitive, and stubbornly unpoetic. It takes place in the second between an injury and an answer. An insult lands across a dinner table, or a courier arrives at dawn with an order of exile. The animal body answers first: the belly contracts, and the mouth fills with the taste of cold copper. Stoicism does not forbid the tremor; it refuses the narrative the mind wants to paste over the nerve. It withholds the second blow. A man sits by the hearth, takes his bread without honey, and waits for the pulse to slow until his fingers are steady enough to mend an old sandal.

Seneca, waiting in his villa while Nero’s horsemen rode south through the dust, suggested that one should occasionally dress in rough wool and drink cistern water, asking: *Is this the misfortune that kept me awake?* The practice has no heroics in it. It resembles nothing so much as salt rubbed into raw pork to keep the rot away. It leaves a person alone with the plain facts of an ordinary afternoon: the iron bucket drawn up dripping from the dark, and the latch clicking into place before the rain starts.
