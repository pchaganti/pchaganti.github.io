# Corrections for the Eye

Pentelic marble contains microscopic veins of iron. Under rain and Mediterranean salt, the metal oxidizes slowly, turning the cold lime of the quarry into the color of dried honey or parched skin. To drag twenty thousand tons of it down Mount Pentelikon required timber sledges, oxen, and ropes soaked in vinegar to keep them from snapping under friction. Up on the limestone tooth of the rock, workmen set the drums with wooden centering plugs, grinding drum against drum with fine sand between them until the joint vanished, leaving a seam so tight that not even a knife point or a drop of tallow could lodge in the fault.

Nothing in the colonnade is actually straight. Had Ictinus built the floor dead level, the human eye, which is a curved lens easily fatigued by geometry, would perceive the middle as sagging under the weight of the sky. The stylobate therefore arches slightly, rising several inches toward its center. The columns do not stand plumb; they lean inward, so that if their lines were projected upward into the air, they would meet nearly two miles above the Attic plain, forming an apex in empty blue light. The corner pillars are visibly thicker than the others, set closer to their neighbors to compensate for the fact that they are silhouetted against bright air rather than the shaded wall of the cella. It is a monument founded on the admission of human flaw: the stone must bend so that our imperfect sight may believe it upright.

For two millennia, the structure absorbed other gods and other disasters. It was a cathedral with a bell tower, then a mosque with a whitewashed apse, before the Venetian mortar shell descended on the Ottoman gunpowder stored inside on a September night in 1687. The blast threw the roof across the terrace and split the marble columns like dried cane. Afterward came the scavenging: collectors chopping away metopes with iron chisels, soldiers melting down the lead collars around the internal cramps to cast musket balls, leaving the bare iron to rust, expand, and fracture the blocks from within.

Near the corner where the architrave meets the western capital, modern restorers now use titanium dowels, which will not rot inside the stone. A mason sits beneath a corrugated plastic roof, tapping an off-white fragment with a flat hammer, wearing a paper mask powdered with limestone dust. The air smells of chipped flint and sun-cured thyme. When the light drops behind Salamis, the shadows stretch along the flutes of the marble, gray and clean as blades, but if you press a palm against the south face of a fallen drum, the stone is still warm, giving back the noon heat like a creature that has only recently laid down.
