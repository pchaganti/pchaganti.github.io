# The Clean Cut

Bashō’s traveling bag held an inkstone, a writing brush, dried paper mulberry, and perhaps two changes of clothing. A form that lives on so little weight must be built of matter that does not spoil: a crow on a withered bough, cold well water, a melon rind left in the dirt. Seventeen phonetic units are barely enough to clear the throat. In Japanese, they are counted not as syllables but as *on*, sounds of uniform duration, clicking past like dried beans dropped into an empty wooden bowl. The foreign imitation obsessively counts finger-joints to assemble five, seven, five, mistaking the scaffolding for the house. The real engine of the verse is not the metric tally; it is the cut.

In the classic line, this cut (*kireji*) acts with the indifference of kitchen steel. A perception begins—an owl on a roof beam, the heat of late August—and then the blade falls. What follows is not an explanation, but an adjacent fact: the smell of sour mash, or the white belly of a turnip. No bridge connects them. Rhetoric insists that an image explain its companion, that sorrow find an echo in the rain, but the haiku distrusts metaphor. Metaphor is an intrusion of human vanity, anxious to prove that a pine tree resembles an old man or that a cloud shares our grief. A pine tree in a haiku is merely wood and needles, stiff with frost. Issa writes of a snail climbing Mount Fuji, but he does not cheer the creature on; he leaves it upon the slope, damp against the rock.

This refusal to generalize produces an uncomfortable stillness. Western lyric verse prefers the arc of memory, which gathers time into an ache; haiku prefers the instant of impact, before memory has had leisure to arrange the wound. It is an art of peripheral vision, recording what was about to slip into the underbrush unnoticed. A winter daikon pulled from the mud brings its own dirt into the kitchen. The poem exists precisely to record that cold grit under the fingernail, nothing more.

When read aloud, the verse leaves behind a disproportionate silence, like a pebble dropped down a dry well. The ear waits for the remainder of the sentence, the subordinate clause that would balance the scale, but the speaker has already closed the screen door. On the page, the ink dries quickly. Outside, the ditch water skins over with early ice, and on the edge of the bucket, a wasp drinks before the dark takes it.
