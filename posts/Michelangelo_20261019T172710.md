# The Horn of Tallow

He went to Carrara not as a surveyor of landscapes, but as a contractor of bulk weight. He spent eight months among the quarrymen, watching them hammer iron wedges into drilled seams until the mountainside cracked along its fault. Marble did not suggest spirit to him until it had been dragged down to the coast on timber rollers greased with pig fat. In his letters, money is never an abstraction. It is silver ducats counted out on grease-spotted pine, and coarse loaves turned sour in the saddlebag. He slept in his dog-skin boots for weeks at a stretch; when he finally pulled them off, the calloused skin of his heels came away with the hide. By forty, his body was already misshapen from the work: the spine bent backward from ceilings, eyes inflamed by falling lime, urine clouded with gravel from the dust in his throat.

Sculpture, he wrote, proceeds *per forza di levare*—by the force of taking away. What proceeds by adding on, the compliant clay or wax, he considered an art for the soft-handed. He attacked the block with the claw-toothed *gradina*, leaving parallel scars across the stone that look like rake marks in frozen mud. In the Medici Chapel, the nape of the Virgin’s neck is still crossed by these jagged furrows, rough as a quarry wall. He had no patience for charm. When he carved the dead Christ of his youth, he made the right forearm heavy with thick veins, swollen like the arm of an oarsman. 

At night in his Rome workshop, he wore a cap of thick paper with a candle of goat tallow pinned above his brow. It left both hands free: one to hold the punch, the other to swing the heavy mallet. The single flame swayed as he nodded, throwing a distorted, leaping shadow across the damp walls. From that gloom came the *Prisoners*, their shoulders straining against the mineral envelope. It is customary to find an intentional poetry in their incompletion. But he did not leave them unfinished out of reverence for the rough block. He left them because a dark fault had opened in the grain, or because the Pope’s messengers had arrived with fresh demands, and the stone had simply won.

Near eighty, he turned the hammer on his own work. He attacked the Florentine Pietà in a fury, hacking at Christ’s thigh and left arm because the marble was hard as flint and threw sparks against his iron. A servant gathered the fractured limbs from the floor; another hand later pinned them back together, grafting smooth replacements onto the hacked torso. In the *Rondanini*, which he chipped at six days before his death, there is hardly any weight left to cut away. Mother and son are reduced to two narrow splinters leaning against each other for support, covered in blows that had grown too light to break the mineral.
