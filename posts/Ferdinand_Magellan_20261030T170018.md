# The Salt Reckoning

Fernão de Magalhães dragged his left leg, the keepsake of a Moorish lance in Azemmour. Men who limp carry an intolerable patience; they are already accustomed to the friction of the ground. In Seville, he showed the ministers of the Casa de Contratación a globe painted by Martin Behaim, where the continent of South America narrowed into a passage—a sea gate that existed only because the German geographer had mistaken the freshwater mouth of the Río de la Plata for the end of the earth. On this phantom cleft, Magellan staked five carvel-built hulls and two hundred and seventy men, many of whom hated his Portuguese silence before the hawsers were cast off.

Wintering at San Julián, where the frost split the oak water-casks, he put down a mutiny with cold speed: one captain was stabbed while reading a summons, another beheaded on the gravel, while an insubordinate friar was left behind on the rocks with two pounds of biscuit. Magellan understood that mercy on a barren coast resembles fright. When the true strait finally showed itself—a black crevasse smelling of crushed bladderwrack and wet slate—it did not welcome them. It was a labyrinth of submerged rock. For thirty-eight days the fleet groped between basalt walls, flanked by the fires of invisible nomads on the southern hills, until the swells flattened, the air grew mild, and the ships entered a void that offered no landfall for thousands of leagues.

For three months and twenty days, they saw nothing living but the shipworms boring through the hull timber. The shipboard biscuit had decomposed into a gray powder crawling with weevils and foul with rat dung. Men unlaced the ox-hides from the mainyards, towing them in the sea for days to soften the sun-cured leather before chewing it into pulp. Magellan kept his log in dry, unsparing Portuguese, calculating his dead reckoning by the drag of a knotted cord through the salt water. He was not an adventurer seduced by wonder; he was an administrative fanatic who believed that geometry could outlast scurvy, convinced the Spice Islands lay just behind the next grey morning.

The catastrophe at Mactan was almost clerical in its carelessness. Having entangled his men in the local feud of an island chieftain who feigned baptism to gain Spanish muskets, Magellan waded ashore into the coral shallows with fewer than fifty soldiers. An arrow pierced his crippled leg; an iron-tipped bamboo staff drove into his throat. He did not die on an open sea, but in two feet of warm, turbid water, hacked apart while his boats pushed off without him. Eighteen survivors brought the leaking *Victoria* back to the quays of Sanlúcar, ballast heavy with cloves. Magellan remained behind in the reef mud, unnamed bones in an unmapped bay, having demonstrated the circumference of the earth by perishing in its vast, indifferent middle.
