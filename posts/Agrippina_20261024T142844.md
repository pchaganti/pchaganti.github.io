# The Iron in the Silt

The Bay of Naples that night was without wind, the surface of the Tyrrhenian black and thick as poured oil. Nero had built a vessel of gilded cedar, designed to splinter along concealed joints when a ceiling weighted with lead dropped into the master cabin. The engineering failed only because the frame of a couch proved stouter than the rigging. While her companion Acerronia cried out in the darkness that she was the Emperor’s mother—and was promptly beaten under water with setting-poles—Agrippina kept silence and swam with a bruised shoulder toward the oyster beds of Lucrinus. 

She knew the machinery of the Palatine better than the men who held the ledger. Power, for the Claudian line, was never an abstract claim; it was a physical residue. It was the smear of colocynth on Claudius’s silver dish of boletus, or the milk she had pressed into the mouth of the boy who now sent rowers to drown her. She had survived Caligula’s exile on Pontia by gathering sea-urchins with raw fingers; she had outflanked Messalina by presenting herself to Claudius until the Senate obligingly voted incest into civil law. She did not mistake violence for an aberration. It was merely domestic administration carried out with heavier utensils.

In the villa at Bauli, having washed the salt from her skin, she lay under a single terracotta lamp. The room smelled of wet plaster and stale fish oil. When the door gave way, it was not the praetorian guard who entered, but Anicetus, the freedman tutor whom she had once ordered to grease the axles of her litters. He brought two sailors behind him. They carried an oar-club and a Spanish blade with a chipped point. The lone serving girl slipped through the drapery, leaving Agrippina with the noise of nailed sandals scuffing lime from the floor.

Tacitus writes that she drew back her tunic and commanded the centurion to drive the iron into her womb, naming the place where Nero had coiled before he learned to breathe. Whether she spoke the phrase or whether the freedman struck her before she could find her voice belongs to the dust of rhetoric. What remains certain is the cheap pyre built hours later on an ordinary dining couch, the yellow smoke fed with trimmed laurel and pine splinters, while the young Emperor waited in Baiae for the courier to wash the grease from his hands.
