# The Lentil and the Dog

Diogenes carried his home on his knuckles and knees. The vessel was not timber—that was a Roman mistake—but an earthenware storage jar, large enough for pickled fish or dried figs, wedged near the temple of Cybele in the Athenian Agora. It smelled of brine and damp clay. When a mongrel approached, he did not shoo it; he noted the hinge of its hip joints, the way it swallowed gristle without wiping its chin, and how it slept where the stone held the heat of the midday sun. His exile from Sinope had begun with adulterating the city coinage, scraping the bronze face of the magistrate down to tin. He treated philosophy as that exact labor: scratching the gilding off the polis until the raw beast showed through the civic fat.

Plato called him a Socrates gone mad, but Diogenes was merely Socrates stripped of his clean tunic and his dinner invitations. While the Academy parsed the metaphysical perimeter of the Good, Diogenes sat in the dust eating salted lupins, spitting the grey skins into the robes of passing rhetoricians. When Plato defined man as a featherless biped, Diogenes plucked a cockerel, dropped the naked, squawking carcass onto the lecture floor, and walked away. There was no syllogism to refute; there was only a stripped bird shivering on marble. He pissed against the marketplace pillars not because he was furious, but because shame required walls, and walls invited thieves. 

Alexander stood above him at the Craneion, casting the broad shadow of a crested helmet. The young king offered him anything under heaven; the beggar asked him to step out of the morning warmth. It was not a grand gesture of defiance, which is only another garment, but an assessment of temperature. A man needs the sun on his ribs and perhaps a handful of parched peas; kings possess territory, which cannot be digested, and titles, which do not soften gravel under an old elbow. Diogenes owned a wooden bowl until he saw a child scoop spring water from the ditch with cupped palms. He threw the cup into the mud, disgusted that he had hauled useless lumber for so many years.

When asked how he wished to be buried, he said outside the town ditch, with a staff left near his hand to beat off the wolves. His companions objected that he would be dead, unconscious, unable to swing the pine branch. *Then why should I care what eats me?* he answered. One thinks of his molars, worn yellow and flat by unbolted grain, and the dust settling into the creases of his neck while Corinth argued about whether virtue was an art or a habit. A stray bitch circles his heap of wool, sniffs the hem, and finds nothing worth stealing.
